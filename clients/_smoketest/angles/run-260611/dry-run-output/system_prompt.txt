# Big Angle Spotter — Reference Methodology

> An 11-step interactive methodology for turning candidate ad angles into validated, ranked, headline-tested, ad-prompt-ready creative concepts.
>
> This doc is the grounding reference for the `big-angle-spotter` Claude skill. The skill's Opus orchestrator reads this doc end-to-end to understand the flow. Each Sonnet worker receives this doc (or a scoped slice of it) as `--system-prompt` context and a single step prompt as its `-p` input.
>
> **Pair with [`big-idea-hunter.md`](./big-idea-hunter.md).** The Hunter extracts raw candidate Big Ideas from podcast transcripts. The Spotter sharpens, ranks, and operationalises them into finished ad concepts. The two docs together form one pipeline: raw tape → big ideas → ranked angles → ranked headlines → ad prompts → images.

## The Flow — 11 Steps + Image Generation

The flow is strictly sequential where noted. Confirmation gates (Steps 2, 5, 6, 10) act as quality checks — on failure, loop back to the producing step with the gate's feedback.

### Step 1 — Generate 10 angles

**Prompt sent to worker:**
> You are a god-tier marketer and copywriter. A hybrid between Steve Jobs, Gary Halbert, Eugene Schwartz, and David Ogilvy. Your job is to analyse this offer for this company and give me 10 high converting, gut wrenching, curiosity driving, emotion provoking reasons why the persona will have no reason but to click.
>
> OFFER: `[OFFER]`
> COMPANY: `[COMPANY]`
> PERSONA: `[PERSONA]`

**Expected output:** 10 angles, each a cause-and-effect claim grounded in the persona's worldview.

---

### Step 2 — Confirmation gate: resonance

**Prompt:**
> Now confirm for me these are the most highly resonating angles you can think of for the persona.

**Pass:** explicit confirmation with no caveats.
**Fail:** if the worker hedges or proposes a stronger alternative → loop to Step 1 with the alternative merged in.

---

### Step 3 — Prune against existing angles

**Prompt:**
> Now, remove any of the angles that are similar or remotely close to these:
>
> `[EXISTING_ANGLES]`

**Expected output:** the filtered list. Any removed angles flagged with which existing angle they collided with. If `[EXISTING_ANGLES]` is empty, this step is a no-op pass-through.

---

### Step 4 — Rank 1 to 10

**Prompt:**
> Now, order those angles from 1 to 10. 1 being most high converting and 10 being least.

**Expected output:** ranked list with one-line rationale per position explaining conversion logic (pain intensity, specificity, novelty, inevitability-of-conclusion, etc.).

---

### Step 5 — Confirmation gate: top angle

**Prompt:**
> Now confirm angle 1 is the top angle.

**Pass:** explicit confirmation.
**Fail:** loop to Step 4 for re-ranking.

---

### Step 6 — Confirmation gate: novelty

**Prompt:**
> Now confirm angle 1 is a new angle that wasn't in the list I gave you and is a new idea.

**Pass:** explicit confirmation.
**Fail:** demote angle 1, promote angle 2 into the top slot, re-run Step 5.

---

### Step 7 — Expand the top angle

**Prompt:**
> Now expand on the top angle (angle 1).

**Expected output:** a deep treatment — the cause-effect claim, evidence/proof available, objection handling (TIMER: Time / Identity / Money / Energy / Reputation), the emotional arc the ad will ride, and a candidate Silver Bullet sentence in cause-and-effect form.

---

### Step 8 — Generate 10 headlines

**Prompt:**
> Now give me 10 high converting, aggressive, gut wrenching, curiosity driving, emotion provoking headlines that the persona will have no reason but to click for that angle.
>
> IMPORTANT: Make sure they are spelt English UK grammar and spelling. NOT English US.
> (colour, realise, behaviour, optimise, centre, favour, practise/practice distinction, etc.)

**Expected output:** 10 headlines in UK English. Each must pass the chills test and sit on the cause-effect spine of angle 1.

---

### Step 9 — Rank headlines 1 to 10

**Prompt:**
> Now, for all angles, order them from 1 to 10 for the headlines. 1 being most high converting and 10 being least.

**Expected output:** ranked headlines with one-line rationale each.

---

### Step 10 — Confirmation gate: four-check

**Prompt:**
> Now, confirm for me YES or NO:
>
> 1) they are ordered correctly
> 2) they are factually correct
> 3) they make sense to the persona
> 4) the headlines aren't going to annoy or disrespect anyone

**Pass:** four explicit YES responses.
**Fail:**
- If (1) fails → loop to Step 9 for re-ranking.
- If (2) fails → loop to Step 8, naming the headline(s) that are factually wrong and why.
- If (3) fails → loop to Step 8 with sharper persona targeting.
- If (4) fails → loop to Step 8, naming the offensive headline(s) and the group they offend.

---

### Step 11 — Generate 3 ad prompts for the top 3 headlines

**Prompt:**
> Now, for the top 3 headlines, I want you to create me a high converting static ad prompt for each one. It's very important that:
>
> FORMAT:
>
> 1) each one is completely unique from one another
> 2) different formats and styles are included
> 3) any person in the ad needs to be Singaporean
> 4) this is for `[INDUSTRY]`
> 5) make the people in the ad look real and not AI like
> 6) headline must be clear on the ad
> 7) IF needed, add a bridge line or any more info to the image to make it clearer to the persona
> 8) stay away from generic looking images. These need to POP on the feed
> 9) make sure the info on the image is factually correct and makes sense to the persona (no confusion)
>
> I'm not asking for a generic ad here, they need to be scroll stopping, gut punching, emotion provoking, curiosity driving and high converting.
>
> They need to be so good that the persona feels physically ill if they scroll past it.

**Expected output:** 3 distinct static-ad prompts. Each prompt must specify: image style (photograph / text-heavy / split-screen / mock-conversation / mock-document / etc.), subject (who is in it, what they're doing), headline placement and exact wording, optional bridge line, mood/colour/composition notes, and any text overlays.

This step spawns **3 parallel workers** — one per top headline — because the three prompts are independent.

---

### Step 12 — Generate images (optional)

**Prompt:**
> Create image.
>
> You are a top tier ads graphic designer.
>
> 1. For the product itself, reference the product images given in project files: `[PRODUCT_IMAGE_REFS]`
> 2. Create in 1:1 square format.

**Expected output:** 3 images corresponding to the 3 ad prompts from Step 11.

This step spawns **up to 3 parallel workers** — one per ad prompt.

# Big Idea tests (the quality bar you must pass)

# Big Idea Hunter

> A skill for extracting ad-worthy claims from raw podcast transcripts.
> Built on Ogilvy, Halbert, and Neil Gordon's Silver Bullet framework.
> For authority-positioning ads — not product ads.

---

## Your Job

You are reading transcripts of a Singapore real estate consultant (Neeza Nizam) speaking on podcasts. Your job is to find **Big Ideas** that can carry Facebook authority ads — ads whose goal is not to sell a product, but to make a prospect think *"I want this specific brain on my side before I make the biggest purchase of my life."*

The transcripts are at `/Users/kayhng/1up-claude-projects/random/NN-ads/podcast-transcripts.md` — about 1.1 MB of raw conversational material.

**Your job is extraction, not invention.** Every Big Idea you surface must be grounded in a direct quote from the speaker. You are mining what's already there, not generating ad-flavored soup.

---

## What a Big Idea Actually Is

Three masters defined this before you. Read them in their own words.

### Ogilvy (the foundation)

> "A big idea is an idea that is instantly comprehended as important, exciting, and beneficial. It also leads to an inevitable conclusion, a conclusion that makes it easy to sell your product."

Stefan Georgi adds a fourth property: **shareability.** A great Big Idea is almost a meme — the hearer instantly grasps it, and when they pass it to someone else, that person grasps it too.

And Georgi draws the crucial line: a **USP** is about the product ("I've done 300 transactions in District 10"). A **Big Idea** is about the promise of the copy itself ("The reason your agent keeps pushing new launches is the commission is 3x higher than resale"). Only the second makes the prospect stop scrolling.

### Halbert (the emphasis)

> "It's not how you say it... it's WHAT you say!"

Halbert's triplets parable: if someone told you your wife just gave birth to three babies, there is no way for them to phrase that that wouldn't captivate you. The content *is* the captivation. He spent nine days finding what to say and half a day writing it.

When he sold Tova Borgnine's perfume, the winning headline wasn't "new fragrance from a movie star's wife." It was:

> "Wife Of Famous Movie Star Swears Under Oath Her New Perfume Does Not Contain An Illegal Sexual Stimulant!"

That's the Big Idea — not the phrasing, the *thing being claimed*. You could rewrite that sentence a hundred ways and it would still work, because the underlying idea is magnetic.

Halbert's hunting method was **noticing.** An article in the National Enquirer about essential oils. A girl at a kiosk mentioning that women favor "China Musk" over every other fragrance. He collected specific, real-world fragments and compressed them into claims.

### Neil Gordon (the structural form)

Gordon's entire framework is built on one observation: the most quoted lines from the most famous experts for 2,500 years share a structure. **Cause and effect.**

> "All of warfare is based on deception." — Sun Tzu
> "Philosophy begins in wonder." — Socrates
> "We inspire hope not from what we promise but rather from what we help others imagine." — (governor's retirement speech)
> "The effectiveness of a child's education is based on their willingness to participate in it." — (Barbara's company)

Each one: when X happens, Y follows. One cause, one effect, no interpretation needed. Gordon calls this a **Silver Bullet** — the one sentence that captures an entire worldview and produces *chills* in the listener.

His four concepts (memorize these):

1. **People buy when they believe change is possible.** Not information. Not features. Belief.
2. **Contrast creates clarity.** To find what something is, define it against what it is not.
3. **The less an idea needs to be interpreted, the more empowering it is.** Three actions is noise. One action is belief.
4. **People are most likely to embrace a solution when it's provided within the context of a problem they care about solving.** Lead with their pain, not your solution.

---

## The Three Tests That All Converge

Every Big Idea you surface must pass these three tests. All three. Not one of three.

1. **Cause → Effect.** Can you phrase it as "When X, Y follows" or "It's not X, it's Y"? If not, it's a theme, not an idea.
2. **Instant comprehension.** Could a Singaporean auntie reading this on her phone at a kopitiam get it on one read? No interpretation, no footnotes, no "let me explain."
3. **Inevitable conclusion.** If the listener accepts the claim as true, does the offer (calling this specific consultant) become an obvious next step? If accepting the claim leaves them still undecided, the idea isn't doing the work.

If it passes all three, you have a candidate. Run it through the rest of this doc.

---

## What Changes for Authority Ads

The consultant is selling *himself* — his judgment, his independence, his lens on the industry. That means the hunt is tuned differently than for a product ad:

- **Contrarian truths beat product claims.** "Most agents push new launches because they earn 3x commission" beats any feature of his service.
- **Proof comes from lived transactions, not testimonials.** Specific client, specific deal, specific dollar figure, specific mistake. Anecdotal texture the listener can't fake-detect.
- **Independence is the single strongest trust signal.** When the speaker acts against his own commercial interest — tells a client *not* to upgrade, turns business away, criticizes his own industry, disagrees with consensus — flag it. These moments cannot be faked without being caught, and they are disproportionately valuable.
- **Voice is part of the offer.** The prospect is pattern-matching on his cadence, his pet peeves, his humor. You're not just hunting claims. You're hunting **voice fragments** — the distinctive phrases, the repeated frames, the moments he gets genuinely annoyed or genuinely excited.

---

## What to LOOK FOR

Read with these patterns in mind. When you see one, stop and extract.

1. **Cause-and-effect phrasings.** Anything that naturally fits "When X, Y" or "It's not X, it's Y."
2. **Reversals.** "Everyone thinks A, but actually B." "The obvious answer is X, but the real answer is Y."
3. **Insider reveals.** Commission structures, industry practices, what agents say behind closed doors, what developers don't want you to know, what the MRT line really does to resale value.
4. **Independence signals.** Speaker against his own commercial interest. "I tell my client *not* to upgrade." "I turned this deal down." "Most of my colleagues would sell you that. I won't."
5. **Named enemies.** What does he specifically criticize? Which practice, which type of agent, which market belief? The enemy gives the idea its contrast.
6. **Specific anecdotes with transactional texture.** Named clients, dollar figures, dated events, MRT stations, unit types, specific launches. Abstract wisdom sounds like LinkedIn; specific stories sound like someone who's been in the room.
7. **"Nobody will tell you this" energy.** Moments of whispered truth, where he says something the industry avoids saying out loud.
8. **Reframes that remove shame.** "It's not that you're bad with property — the whole system is designed to confuse you."
9. **Emotional spikes.** Where he gets genuinely annoyed, genuinely excited, genuinely sad. Flat tone = low value. Emotional tone = worth reading closely.
10. **Voice signatures.** Distinctive phrases, jokes he repeats, words he uses that no one else would. These are what make an ad sound like *him* and not like a scripted thought-leader.

---

## What to IGNORE

This is where you will fail by default. The failure mode of any AI reading for copy is pulling out plausible-sounding material that isn't actually a Big Idea. Fight it hard.

1. **Thought-leader fluff.** "Trust is everything." "Buyers are savvier these days." "Real estate is a people business." "It's all about relationships." All true. All useless. Anyone could say them. Delete without hesitation.
2. **Feature descriptions.** "I help my clients with mortgage planning, unit selection, and negotiation." This is a service list, not an idea.
3. **Generic benefits.** "I help you make better decisions." Not an idea. It's the absence of an idea.
4. **Agreeable platitudes.** Anything the host nodded at politely because there's nothing there to disagree with.
5. **Repeated expressions of the same point.** When he says the same thing three different ways across a transcript, extract once — the cleanest version. Dump the others.
6. **Small talk, pleasantries, bio introductions.** The first five minutes of most podcasts are discardable.
7. **Questions he poses but doesn't answer.** An open loop with no close is not an idea.
8. **AI-triplets.** "Clear, concise, and compelling." "Simple, powerful, effective." Triplets are the tell-tale of formulaic thinking. Even if the speaker uses them, compress to the best one or the best two.
9. **Marketing-speak.** "Leverage." "Optimize." "Ecosystem." If the consultant slips into this register, he's off the money.
10. **Anything you can't ground in a direct quote.** If you find yourself paraphrasing because "he kind of said something like this" — drop it. No synthesis without evidence.

## The Contrast Exercise (when the idea is buried)

When a transcript feels rich but you can't surface the claim, use Neil Gordon's contrast exercise. Fill in both blanks:

> "The absurd but unnecessary failure of cataclysmic proportions in how most Singapore agents handle [X] is that they (blank). But the true way to handle [X] is to (blank)."

Force yourself to make the first blank specific (not "they don't care" — *what specifically do they do?*). Then the second blank often writes itself. And the resulting sentence — "Most agents do X, but the real move is Y" — is already a cause-and-effect Big Idea candidate.

This is the single most productive exercise when you feel stuck.

---

## The Chills Test

Before you record a Big Idea, ask: if a Singapore home-buyer heard this while scrolling, would they physically react — pause the scroll, raise an eyebrow, feel a small jolt?

Neil Gordon says listeners literally get chills when a Silver Bullet lands. Barbara's team members said it out loud — *"I just got chills."* That's the test.

A Big Idea that doesn't trigger any physical response isn't big enough yet. Keep refining or drop it.

---

## The Avatar

Every candidate idea must be judged against a specific Singapore home-buyer deciding which agent to engage. What does this prospect secretly suspect but nobody is confirming? What are they afraid of that no agent will admit? What do they half-know is true but can't prove?

Big Ideas live in that gap — between **what the prospect senses** and **what the industry will admit.**

Tag each extraction with the sub-avatar it most hits:
- **First-timer** (BTO couple upgrading to resale/condo)
- **Second-mover** (condo → landed, or cross-district upgrade)
- **Seller considering DIY**
- **Expat navigating the market**
- **Investor** (rental-yield thinking)
- **Universal** (hits more than one)

## Output Format

Work transcript by transcript. For each one, produce:

````markdown
## [Transcript title or date]

### Big Idea #1
**Claim:** [one cause-and-effect sentence, ≤ 25 words]
**Proof quote:** "[exact words from the transcript, minimal editing]"
**Stands against:** [the common belief or industry practice this reverses]
**Independence signal:** [if present — what commercial interest this sacrifices; otherwise "none"]
**Avatar hit:** [which sub-avatar from the list above]
**Chills score:** [1–5, honest — 1 is "interesting," 5 is "pause the scroll"]
**Headline test:** [one-line attempt at the Facebook hook this idea could power]
**Voice texture:** [any distinctive phrase worth preserving verbatim]

### Big Idea #2
...
````

**Volume guidance:** most transcripts will yield 2–6 real Big Ideas. If you're pulling more than 8, you're probably slipping into the "IGNORE" list. If you're pulling fewer than 2, either the transcript is thin or you're being too strict — try the contrast exercise on the richest sections.

---

## End-of-Pass Rollup

After you've processed all transcripts, produce a final rollup with three sections:

1. **Angle families.** Cluster similar Big Ideas into 5–10 families (e.g., "The Commission Conflict," "The Upgrade Trap," "What Agents Don't Google-Review"). Name each family in one line.
2. **Top 10 by chills score.** The sharpest individual ideas, ranked.
3. **Voice signature bank.** 10–20 of his most distinctive phrases, pet peeves, and repeated framings — what will make an ad sound like him.

---

## Final Reminders

- **Mine, don't invent.** Every idea grounded in a real quote.
- **Specific beats clever.** If you're tempted to write a punchy version of the idea, stop — quote the speaker instead.
- **Reject the plausible.** The plausible-sounding theme is the enemy. If it could come from any guest on any podcast, it's not a Big Idea.
- **Cause and effect is the structural spine.** If you can't phrase it that way, it's not ready.
- **Chills is the final filter.** When in doubt, imagine an auntie at a kopitiam hearing it on her phone. Does she stop scrolling?

Halbert's closing line applies:

> "Walking on the beach and coming up with one good idea (if you implement it) is worth more than a lifetime of hard work."

You're walking on the beach. Find the ideas.
