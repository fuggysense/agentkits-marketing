# Fact-Based Headlines vs Claim-Based Headlines

## What this file is about

The headline is the first line of the letter. The old winning letters used a **specific fact** in the headline — a number, a process, a question about who you are, or a tiny moment from a story. The reader sees the fact and wants to know more.

Most modern letters do the opposite. They make a **claim** about a benefit ("get more leads", "scale your agency"). The reader has heard that claim a hundred times and stops reading.

A fact in the headline makes the reader curious. A claim makes the reader bored.

## The rules

- **Put one specific fact in the headline.** A number, a process step, a question about identity, or a small moment from a real story.
- **Do not lead with the benefit.** "Get more leads" is what the reader wants. It is not why they should read on. The fact is the reason to read on.
- **Numbers must be exact, not round.** "$13,995" feels real. "$14,000" feels rounded. "27.4% return" feels measured. "Big returns" feels made up.
- **If you use a story moment, pick the smallest detail.** Not "I changed my life." But "they laughed when I sat down at the piano."
- **If you use a question, it must be a question about the reader's identity** — their name, their job, their situation. Not a rhetorical question about their pain.
- **Cut every adjective the fact already does for you.** Ogilvy did not write "the amazingly quiet Rolls-Royce." He let the clock fact carry "quiet" on its own.

## Types of fact-headlines

- **The specific-number fact.** One exact number that proves the bigger benefit. *"At 60 miles an hour the loudest noise in this new Rolls-Royce comes from the electric clock." — Ogilvy*
- **The process fact.** A peek at how the thing is made. *"Could you but see how Schlitz beer is brewed, you would never buy any other." — Hopkins*
- **The identity-question fact.** A question naming who the reader is. *"Did you know that your family name was recorded with a coat-of-arms in ancient heraldic archives more than seven centuries ago?" — Halbert*
- **The small-moment story fact.** One vivid second from a story, not the whole story. *"They Laughed When I Sat Down at the Piano… But When I Started to Play!" — Caples*
- **The damaging-admission fact.** A confession that sounds true because nobody would say it to sell. *"I used to work hard. The 18-hour days. The 7-day weeks. But I didn't start making big money until I did less — a lot less." — Karbo*

## Verbatim examples

1. **Ogilvy, Rolls-Royce (1958):** "At 60 miles an hour the loudest noise in this new Rolls-Royce comes from the electric clock." Fact carried: a measured noise reading at a measured speed.
2. **Hopkins, Schlitz (~1919):** "Could you but see how Schlitz beer is brewed, you would never buy any other." Fact carried: there is a brewing process worth seeing.
3. **Caples, U.S. School of Music (1925):** "They Laughed When I Sat Down at the Piano… But When I Started to Play!" Fact carried: one real moment of social humiliation flipping to triumph.
4. **Halbert, Coat of Arms (1971):** "Did you know that your family name was recorded with a coat-of-arms in ancient heraldic archives more than seven centuries ago?" Fact carried: your surname plus a 700-year-old archive.
5. **Karbo, Lazy Man's Way (1973):** "I used to work hard. The 18-hour days. The 7-day weeks. But I didn't start making big money until I did less — a lot less." Fact carried: exact hours worked before the change.

## What modern letters do instead

We looked at the 8 strong modern letters Jerel picked. Most lead with a benefit claim, not a fact. Examples:

- "OPEN THE FLOODGATES OF 'QUALIFIED LOCAL ROOFING JOBS' USING GOOGLE ADS™ SECRETS…" (roofgrow) — claim about lead flow, no fact.
- "How To Grow Your Lawn Care & Landscaping Business To 10M In Revenue Using Our 7 Sales Laws While Experiencing 'Stress Free' Operations" (green-industry) — three claims stacked, no fact.
- "I Will Help You Get A Mortgage APPROVED & CLOSED For Your New Home in 17 Days Or Less…" (brendon-luu) — has one number (17 days), but it is a future promise, not a measured past fact.

The strongest modern example is **george-ten:** "A $50 Facebook ad test will tell you in 48 hours whether your idea can sell." Two exact numbers ($50, 48 hours) and a testable outcome. This headline is closer to the classical pattern than the other seven.

## What reviewers should check

1. **Is there a specific fact in the headline?** A number, a process step, an identity question, or a small story moment. *(yes / no)*
2. **Can the writer point at the fact and name what kind it is?** *(yes / no)*
3. **Are the numbers exact, not rounded?** *(yes / no / no numbers present)*
4. **Does the headline avoid leading with the benefit?** The benefit is implied by the fact, not stated. *(yes / no)*
5. **Could a reader who reads the headline cold feel curious enough to read line two?** Score 1 to 5.
6. **Cut test — if you removed every adjective, would the headline still work?** *(yes / no)*

Checks 1 to 4 catch claim-headlines that should have been fact-headlines. Checks 5 and 6 catch padded headlines that try to do the fact's job with extra words.

## Does this still work in 2026?

Yes. The george-ten letter shows the fact-headline pattern still ships and still pulls. The danger today is that AI-written first drafts default to claim-headlines because that is what the training data is full of. A reviewer must push the writer back to a specific fact every time.

One caveat: in finance and health, every specific number in a headline needs a source you can show. Do not invent a number to sound exact.
