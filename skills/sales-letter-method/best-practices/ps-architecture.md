# The P.S. Block

## What this file is about

The P.S. is the small note at the bottom of a sales letter. Most people skip the middle of a letter. But they read the headline. And they read the P.S. That is why old-school direct response writers worked so hard on it.

Three of the best writers ever — Gary Halbert, Gene Schwartz, and Porter Stansberry — all used the P.S. as a key part of the sale. Halbert put the whole price and order details in the P.S. Schwartz and Stansberry stacked 3 to 5 P.S. notes, each doing a different job.

Today, most long-form sales letters drop the P.S. completely. We looked at 8 strong modern letters from our reference set. All 8 had no P.S. at all. That is a big miss. The old writers proved the P.S. works in real tests. Modern writers stopped using it not because it stopped working, but because the style changed.

So if you are reviewing or writing a sales letter close: a missing P.S. is a real problem, not a style choice.

## The rules

- **Always have at least one P.S.** If a long letter has none, it has dropped a move that has won in real tests for fifty years.
- **Long letters (1,500 words or more) should have 2 to 5 P.S. notes.** One P.S. is okay only if it carries the whole price and order details (the Halbert way).
- **Each P.S. should do its own job.** Never two P.S. notes doing the same thing. If you cannot say what job a P.S. does, cut it.
- **The P.S. is the second-most-read part of the letter, after the headline.** Treat it like prime space. Put the strongest one-line promise, deadline, or risk reversal here.
- **If you only have one P.S., point it at the biggest worry in the reader's head** — the price, the time pressure, the risk, or the fear of being too late.

## Jobs a P.S. can do

When you draft a letter, pick from this list. Do not invent new jobs. Each P.S. should match one of these.

- **The price-and-order P.S.** — Pulls the whole "here is what it costs, here is how to pay" out of the body and parks it at the bottom. This keeps the body about trust, not money. *Halbert, Coat of Arms.*
- **The deadline-restate P.S.** — One line that pushes the time pressure: "offer closes Friday." *Stansberry pattern.*
- **The bonus-reminder P.S.** — Lists the bonuses again so the reader feels the deal is too good to walk away from. *Schwartz pattern.*
- **The guarantee P.S.** — Says the guarantee one more time on its own. *Schwartz pattern.*
- **The "what if I'm not ready" P.S.** — Names the quiet fear most readers have (too old, too late, too new, not smart enough) and shuts it down. *From copy-gems.md.*
- **The "if you do nothing" P.S.** — Paints what happens if the reader walks away and changes nothing. *Stansberry pattern.*
- **The personal-promise P.S.** — A direct line from the writer: "I give you my word." *Halbert-style.*

## The example we have on file

> "P.S. If you are ordering only one report, send two dollars ($2.00). Additional reports ordered at the same time and sent to the same address are one dollar each. Please make checks payable to me, Nancy L. Halbert."
— Halbert, *Coat of Arms*

This one P.S. carries the whole order ask. The price, the math for multiple orders, who to make the check out to. Halbert used it as the price-and-order P.S.

**A note on the other examples.** We do not have clean full-text copies of the Schwartz *Burn Disease* ad or the Stansberry *End of America* letter. The Schwartz one is in a scan we cannot copy text from. The Stansberry one was taken down before we could grab it. But our notes on both letters say they used the multi-P.S. pattern: Schwartz had 3 to 5 P.S. notes doing recap, bonus, and guarantee. Stansberry had several P.S. notes pushing urgency. So treat the other jobs (deadline, bonus, guarantee, fear-handler, do-nothing) as patterns we trust but cannot show word-for-word yet.

## What modern letters are missing

We checked the 8 best modern long-form letters Jerel picked. None of them had any P.S. blocks. Zero.

`copy-gems.md` (line 184) flags this as one of four moves "8 out of 8 modern pages missed — your moat."

What that means for a reviewer: when you grade a modern long letter and there is no P.S., that is the single biggest, easiest fix. It costs one paragraph to add. And it brings back a move the old letters proved works.

## What reviewers should check

Reviewer agents can score yes/no or 1 to 5 on these checks. Score against this list, not against gut feel.

1. **Is there a P.S. at all?** Letter has at least one P.S. block. *(yes / no)*
2. **Right number for the length?** If the letter is 1,500 words or more, there are 2 or more P.S. blocks. *(yes / no / not applicable)*
3. **Each P.S. doing a different job?** If there are multiple P.S. blocks, each does a different job from the list above. No two doing the same thing. *(yes / no / not applicable)*
4. **Single P.S. doing the right job?** If there is only one P.S., it is either the price-and-order P.S. or the "what if I'm not ready" P.S. *(yes / no / not applicable)*
5. **Is the P.S. saying something real?** Score 1 to 5. Is the P.S. carrying a sharp promise, a deadline, a guarantee, a fear-handler? Or is it just a generic sign-off?
6. **Can the writer name the job?** Writer can point at each P.S. and say which job it does. *(yes / no)*

Checks 1 to 4 together replicate what the old winning letters did. Checks 5 and 6 catch padding — P.S. notes added because "we should have one" with no real job.

## Does this still work in 2026?

Mostly yes. Stansberry and Agora kept the multi-P.S. style alive into email and web letters and it still ships today. Two things changed:

- **The mail-order coupon P.S.** (Halbert sending checks in the post) does not work online. The modern version is a P.S. with a button or a link straight to the order page.
- **Health, money, and finance letters** need careful claim language in the P.S., same as in the body. Do not get loose just because it is a P.S.
