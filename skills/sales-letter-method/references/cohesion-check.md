# Cohesion Check

## What this file is about

Cohesion means the reader never feels a bump between sections. The letter sounds like one person talking the whole way through. Every section picks up where the last one left off and answers the question it planted.

When the reader finishes a section thinking *"…and so what?"* and the next section doesn't answer that, the reader is gone.

**Where it runs:** Phase 2 Stitcher, before handoff to Phase 3 Conversion Gate.

---

## The 5 critical boundaries

These five jumps are where readers drop off the hardest. Any `jump` here is an auto-fail.

### 1. Headline → Subheadline (or first paragraph)
The subheadline must answer "Is this real? Who says?" — not start a new topic.

> Halbert opens: *"Did you know that your family name was recorded with a coat-of-arms in ancient heraldic archives more than seven centuries ago?"*
> Next line: *"My husband and I discovered this while doing some research for some friends of ours who have the same last name as you do."*
> — *Halbert, Coat of Arms.* The second sentence answers "who says?" by sourcing the claim back to a real-sounding person.

### 2. Lead → Pain Cycle
The pain section must connect to whatever the lead just promised. Not a topic switch.

> Karbo's lead ends: *"And I'll try to make it so irresistible that you'd be a darned fool not to do it."*
> The pain section opens by listing what his method is *not* — chain letters, stocks, real estate, gambling, positive thinking. Each line answers the question the lead planted: *"okay, what is this actually?"*
> — *Karbo, Lazy Man's Way.*

### 3. Pain Cycle → Mechanism
The mechanism must be the answer to the pain just described.

> Caples builds three paragraphs of laughter, mockery, "Can he really play?" Then a section break and the section heading **Then I Started to Play** — followed by *"Instantly a tense silence fell on the guests."* The mechanism (the playing) is the literal answer to the pain (the mockery).
> — *Caples, Piano.* Best pain-to-mechanism pivot in the file.

### 4. Mechanism → Proof
Proof must show the mechanism working — same words, same frame.

> Halbert names the deliverable: *"this drawing, along with other information about the name, has been printed up into an attractive one-page report."*
> Next paragraph: *"The bottom half of the report tells the story of the very old and distinguished family name of Macdonald."*
> The proof picks up the exact object ("the report") and walks the reader through it.
> — *Halbert, Coat of Arms.*

### 5. CTA → P.S.
The P.S. must answer "am I going to regret this?" — not summarize.

> Halbert's CTA: *"…verify that we have your correct name and address and send the correct amount in cash or check for the number of reports you want."*
> P.S.: *"If you are ordering only one report, send two dollars ($2.00). Additional reports ordered at the same time and sent to the same address are one dollar each."*
> The P.S. answers the unspoken worry: *"wait — how much?"*
> — *Halbert, Coat of Arms.*

## The 4 bridge patterns

When a transition feels jumpy, rewrite it using one of these. Each is shown with a real example.

### Bridge 1 — Echo
Repeat a key word or phrase from the prior section. Anchors the reader.

> *"…discovered this while doing some research for some friends of ours who have the same last name as you do. We've had an artist recreate **the coat-of-arms** exactly as described in the ancient records."*
> — *Halbert.* "Coat-of-arms" carries from headline through three paragraphs.

### Bridge 2 — Escalate
Raise the stakes the prior section set up. Don't reset — push.

> *"…the crowd rocked with laughter."* → **Then I Started to Play** → *"Instantly a tense silence fell on the guests."*
> — *Caples.* The mockery escalates into the reversal in one sentence.

### Bridge 3 — Pivot
Name the turn explicitly. Tell the reader you're changing direction.

> *"It should be remembered that we have not traced anyone's individual family tree but have researched back through several centuries…"*
> — *Halbert.* The "it should be remembered" line is a clean pivot from product description into expectation-setting.

### Bridge 4 — Answer
Treat the next section as the direct answer to the prior section's implied question.

> *"…you'd be a darned fool not to do it."* (reader thinks: "okay, what IS it?") → *"It's not chain letters. It's not stocks, bonds or any other get-rich-quick scheme…"*
> — *Karbo.* The "It's not's" section is structurally an answer.

## What a jumpy transition looks like

Modern long-form letters often paste sections together without bridges. The reader hits a wall.

> Brendon Luu's letter ends pain agitation with a list of fears (credit scores, PMIs, hidden fees), then the next section opens with: *"Book A Home Loan Consultation."*
> No bridge. No answer to "what's the alternative?" before the CTA.
> — *Brendon Luu* (annotated 01-brendon-luu.md flags "no P.S. — leaves a long letter without a final urgency or recap layer").

RoofGrow does the same — case studies stop, then the CTA repeats. There's no Pivot, Echo, or Answer between the proof block and the close. Reader feels the seam.

## What reviewers should check

Score against this list. Yes/no on the boundaries, 1–5 on overall flow.

1. **All 5 critical boundaries clean?** No `jump` at Headline→Sub, Lead→Pain, Pain→Mechanism, Mechanism→Proof, CTA→P.S. *(yes / no)*
2. **Each section opens by echoing, escalating, pivoting, or answering the prior section?** Writer can name which one. *(yes / no per section)*
3. **No transitional filler?** Phrases like "Now let's talk about…", "Moving on…", "Next up…" — flag and cut. *(yes / no)*
4. **No over-bridging?** No section starts with a two-sentence recap of the section before. *(yes / no)*
5. **Overall flow score 1–5.** Read the letter cold, top to bottom. Did you stumble anywhere? Each stumble = minus one point.
6. **Cohesion ratio.** Across all section boundaries, `continuous` + `bridge` ≥ 85% to pass, ≥ 95% for strong, 100% for premium.
