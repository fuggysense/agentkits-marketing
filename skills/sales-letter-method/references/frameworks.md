# Classic Frameworks — Schwartz, Halbert, Sugarman

## What this file is about

Three writers wrote most of the long-form letters that still get studied today: Eugene Schwartz, Gary Halbert, and Joe Sugarman. Each one named a pattern you can copy.

- **Schwartz** named the six ways to open a letter (lead types). Pick one before you write the lead.
- **Halbert** named the way to start a letter so it does not feel like an ad ("Dear avatar" opener).
- **Sugarman** named the way each sentence should pull the reader into the next one (the slippery slide).

This file gives you the verbatim example for each, when to use it, and what reviewers should check. Other files cover the rest of the letter — see the links at the bottom.

---

## Schwartz — the six lead types

Schwartz taught that every letter opens in one of six ways: **offer, promise, problem-solution, big-secret, proclamation, or story.** Pick the one that fits how aware your reader is. Cold readers need story or big-secret. Warm readers can take a direct promise or offer.

**Verbatim example — big-secret + promise lead.** Schwartz's *Burn Disease* ad ran as a control for twenty years. The headline:

> "How modern Chinese Medicine helps Burn Disease Out of Your Body"
> — *Schwartz, Burn Disease.* Source: `research/classical-exemplars/annotated/schwartz-burn-disease.md` (lead typed as "Big-secret + promise + named mechanism").

The lead does three jobs in one line: it teases a hidden method (big-secret), promises a visceral outcome (burn disease out), and names the mechanism (modern Chinese Medicine).

**When to use which lead type:**

- **Story or big-secret** — cold reader, has not heard of you, low trust. Most modern sales letters need this.
- **Problem-solution or promise** — reader already knows they have the problem. Skip the setup.
- **Offer or proclamation** — reader knows you and is close to buying. Rare in cold traffic.

If you do not know which one to pick, default to story. Caples' piano ad (see Sugarman section below) opens with a story headline and it has been copied for a hundred years.

## Halbert — the "Dear avatar" opener

Halbert sent six hundred million pieces of mail using one trick: open the letter the way a friend would. Name the reader. No company logo at the top. No "Dear Customer." A real name.

**Verbatim example:**

> "Dear Mr. Macdonald,
>
> Did you know that your family name was recorded with a coat-of-arms in ancient heraldic archives more than seven centuries ago?"
> — *Halbert, Coat of Arms.* Source: `research/classical-exemplars/raw/halbert-coat-of-arms.md`.

The opener does two jobs. It uses the reader's actual name (Macdonald, not "friend"). And the first sentence is a question about *them*, not a pitch about the product.

**When to use it:** any cold letter where the reader does not know you yet. Works for personalised email, direct mail, and any letter where you can name a real avatar segment (not "entrepreneurs" — "burned-out roofers in Texas").

**When not to use it:** if you cannot name the avatar tightly, the opener falls flat. "Dear friend" reads as a mass mailer. Better to use a Schwartz story lead instead.

See also: `cohesion-check.md` section 1 (Headline → Subheadline) — the same Halbert letter is used there to show how the second line should answer "who says?"

## Sugarman — the slippery slide

Sugarman's rule: every sentence has one job, which is to make the reader read the next sentence. The first sentence is short. The second is short. By the time the reader has read three short sentences, they are sliding down the page and cannot stop.

**Verbatim example — Caples' piano ad opens this way:**

> "They Laughed When I Sat Down at the Piano… But When I Started to Play!"
> — *Caples, U.S. School of Music.* Source: `research/classical-exemplars/annotated/caples-piano.md`.

The headline is the start of the slide. The body keeps it going:

> "Arthur had just played 'The Rosary.' The room rang with applause."
> — *Caples, same letter.*

Short sentences. Each one sets up the next. The reader is inside the scene by sentence two.

**When to use it:** always. The slippery slide is not a choice — it is the test every letter has to pass. If your reader can stop after sentence three without wanting to read sentence four, the letter is broken.

**The rule:** the first sentence of any section should be five words or fewer if you can manage it. Then the next sentence answers the question the first one planted.

See also: `cohesion-check.md` — the slippery slide applies inside a paragraph; cohesion-check covers the same pattern between sections.

## What reviewers should check

Score yes or no on each. Two no's means the letter is not built on a winning pattern yet.

1. **Lead type named.** Writer can point at the opener and say which Schwartz lead type it is (offer, promise, problem-solution, big-secret, proclamation, or story).
2. **Opener names a real avatar.** First line either uses the reader's name, or names the segment tightly enough that the wrong reader would walk away. Not "Dear friend."
3. **First sentence is short.** Five words or fewer if possible. Ten max. Long opening sentences kill the slide before it starts.
4. **Each sentence sets up the next.** Read three sentences cold. If you can stop after sentence two and not feel pulled, the slide is broken.
5. **Lead matches reader awareness.** Cold reader gets story or big-secret. Warm reader can take promise or offer. If a cold reader hits an offer lead, the letter loses them.
