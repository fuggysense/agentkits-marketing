# Qualification Patterns

## What qualification actually does

Qualification is the part of the letter that tells the wrong reader to leave. That sounds bad. It is the move that makes the right reader lean in.

When the seller filters, the buyer relaxes. A letter that tries to sell everyone reads like a pitch. A letter that says "this is for X, not for Y" reads like an honest person picking their work.

Real qualification names something testable — money, stage, time, situation. Vague qualification ("for serious people only") does nothing. The reader cannot tick yes or no, so it filters nobody.

## Named patterns from the research

### 1. The pain-persona checklist
Syncom's "PLEASE READ THIS LETTER IF:" block. A list of specific painful moments, each one a checkbox the reader either nods at or skips.

Verbatim, Syncom mortgage letter:
> "PLEASE READ THIS LETTER IF:
> ❌ You need a loan from the bank but they are turning you down
> ❌ You have enough equity in your home but your bank still refuses to give you a loan
> ❌ You're having a tough time making mortgage payments and you feel worried
> ❌ You're at the risk of losing your property due to a power of sale and it's causing stress
> ❌ You have large credit card or other personal debts, and you URGENTLY need a loan..."
— `strong-exemplars/raw/06-syncom-crK2.md`

Strength: every line is concrete. The reader either lives it or does not.
Already documented as a copy gem — see `copy-gems.md` "Pain-Persona Checklist."

### 2. The identity-question hook
Halbert's Coat of Arms letter qualifies by family name, not by pain:
> "Did you know that your family name was recorded with a coat-of-arms in ancient heraldic archives more than seven centuries ago?"
— `classical-exemplars/annotated/halbert-coat-of-arms.md`

The reader self-selects. If your surname is on the merge tag, you read on. If it is missing, you bin it. Identity-targeted, not pain-targeted.

### 3. The implicit-affordability filter
Ogilvy's Rolls-Royce never says "this is for rich people." The headline does that work for him:
> "At 60 miles an hour the loudest noise in this new Rolls-Royce comes from the electric clock."
— `classical-exemplars/annotated/ogilvy-rolls-royce.md`

The car costs $13,995. The reader who cannot spend that walks away on their own. No disqualifier needed. Use when the offer's price is visible and the audience self-filters by it.

### 4. The exclusivity gate
Frank Kern's "Do-It-For-You Service" sets a $100 deposit before talks even start. Per Schauer's commentary on the swipe:
> "The deposit plays several KEY roles. First, it does indeed eliminate the non-serious. Two, it makes the person more likely to buy because they already made a small commitment & three, makes the deal seem a lot more exclusive."
— `swiped-segmented/info-products/video-sales-letter-do-it-for-you-service-from-frank-kern.md`

Dan Kennedy's Renegade Millionaire letter does the same thing with language, calling the offer a "radical underground" and "secret society" (Schauer's reading) so only readers who want to join an inside group continue. *(Note: "radical underground" and "secret society" are Schauer's paraphrase, not verified verbatim from Kennedy's letter.)* See `swiped-segmented/info-products/original-renegade-millionaire-salesletter-from-dan-kennedy.md`.

Use when the offer is high-ticket and you want the reader to feel they are applying, not buying.

### 5. The story-as-mirror filter
Caples' piano ad never disqualifies anyone outright. The story does the filtering:
> "They Laughed When I Sat Down at the Piano… But When I Started to Play!"
— `classical-exemplars/annotated/caples-piano.md`

Readers who relate to the social-shame-then-vindication moment lean in. Others do not. Use when the offer is identity-shift and the reader needs to see themselves in a scene before they will buy.

## When to qualify

- **Info-products and agency offers** — heavy qualification. The pain-persona checklist is now table stakes in this niche (4 of 8 strong modern letters in the corpus use it).
- **High-ticket consulting** — use the exclusivity gate. Deposit, application, cap on slots.
- **Luxury or premium price-visible products** — implicit-affordability filter is enough. Do not add a "not for you" block; the price already filters.
- **Health, finance, legal** — careful with explicit disqualification. Disqualifying by income or condition can read as predatory or even cross compliance lines. Stay with situation-based filters.

## What weak qualification looks like

- **Vague filler.** "This is for ambitious agents who want to grow." Nothing testable. The reader cannot tick yes or no.
- **Flattery disguised as a filter.** "This isn't for you if you're not committed to success." Nobody will tick that box, so nobody is filtered out.
- **Insults pretending to be filters.** "Not for lazy people." Pushes good readers away because they hate the tone, not the offer.
- **Conflict with the headline.** Headline says "anyone can do this." Then the body says "this isn't for beginners." Reader catches the lie and leaves.
- **Template overfit.** The Syncom corpus shows the same "PLEASE READ THIS LETTER IF:" block recycled across 4 letters with near-identical wording (loans, mortgages, business funding). When every letter in a niche uses the same template, the move stops feeling like honest filtering and starts feeling like a funnel pattern. See `strong-exemplars/_summary.md` calibration warning.

## What reviewers should check

1. **Is there a qualification block at all?** A letter with no filter reads as "we will take anyone's money."
2. **Are the lines testable?** Each one should be a thing the reader can tick yes or no on with no thinking — money, stage, time, situation.
3. **Is there at least one real "not for you" line?** Saying who it is not for is the strongest trust signal in the letter. See `damaging-admission.md` — admitting limits is a form of damaging admission.
4. **Does the filter match the headline?** If the headline promises something for anyone and the filter says "only for X" — one of them is wrong.
5. **Is the tone honest filtering or gatekeeping theatre?** Honest reads like a founder protecting delivery. Theatre reads like a doorman.
6. **For consulting-level offers — is there a readiness check before the CTA?** Reframes the click as "am I ready?" not "do I want this?"
