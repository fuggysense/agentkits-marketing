# Trust Density

## What this file is about

Trust is built by what you can show, not what you say about yourself. The old greats packed their letters with small, checkable facts — what the ad cost, how many coats of paint were on a Rolls-Royce, the exact path the water took through a brewery. Modern letters do not. They lean on testimonials, headshots, and the word "transparent" — and the reader's guard goes up because no real ground has been given.

Trust density is the count of those small, checkable signals across a letter. Aim for at least **5 different kinds of signal, spread across at least 3 different parts of the letter.** Below that and it reads as a pitch. Above that and the proof stops feeling planted — it feels like a case.

## The trust signals

Each signal is a different *kind* of proof. Pick from this list. Do not double up two of the same kind.

- **The verifiable-fact signal.** A specific, checkable claim about the product or process. Not a feeling, not a promise — a fact a sceptic could go and check.
  > "At 60 miles an hour the loudest noise in this new Rolls-Royce comes from the electric clock."
  — Ogilvy, *Rolls-Royce*. The body that follows lists 13 more facts of the same kind: "seven hours at full throttle", "98 separate ordeals", "5 coats of primer", "9 coats of finishing paint", "three-year guarantee".

- **The demonstration signal.** Walk the reader through how the product is actually made or run. Show the machinery.
  > "Every pump and pipe cleaned twice daily… every bottle washed four times by machinery… 4,000-foot artesian wells… 1,200 experiments."
  — Hopkins, *Schlitz*. Every brewery did this. Hopkins was the first to take the reader inside the plate-glass rooms and the filtered air. He owned "pure" because he was first to *show* it.

- **The damaging-admission signal.** Give up a small piece of ground. See `best-practices/damaging-admission.md` for the full pattern.
  > "I'm going to ask you to send me ten dollars for something that'll cost me no more than 50 cents."
  — Karbo, *Lazy Man's Way to Riches*. The reader's guard drops. Every other claim becomes more believable.

- **The named-source signal.** Quote a named third party with a checkable title — not "industry experts say".
  > Ogilvy quoted "the Technical Editor of THE MOTOR" by title. Stansberry cited Niall Ferguson and Marc Faber by name. The reader can verify the source exists.

- **The specific-number signal.** A number the writer would only know if they had counted. Specificity *is* credibility.
  - "3,247 appointments" beats "thousands of appointments".
  - "$69/day ad spend" beats "minimal spend".
  - Karbo: "this ad took about two hours to write." Two hours, not "a couple of hours".

- **The constraint signal.** State what the offer can *not* do.
  - "This doesn't replace your listing agent. It replaces the cold-lead chase."
  - "Most clients see first results in 30–60 days. Some take 90. Nobody sees traction in week one."

- **The conditions-for-success signal.** Name what has to be true on the reader's side for the method to work.
  - "This works if you can reply to inbound leads within 2 hours. If your reply time is 24+ hours, none of this matters — fix that first."

- **The identity-anchor signal.** A fact tied to who the reader *is*, not how they feel. Halbert's *Coat of Arms* led with: "Did you know that your family name was recorded with a coat-of-arms in ancient heraldic archives more than seven centuries ago?" The hook lands because the reader's surname is not a guess.

- **The past-prediction signal.** Past calls the writer made, with dates, that turned out right. Stansberry listed his correct calls with dates — proving forecasting, not just current revenue. Stream A authors instead list current revenue ("$2M in 12 months") which only proves luck.

## Density calibration

A long letter should carry at least **5 different signals across at least 3 different parts** (lead, body, proof block, close). If they all sit in one testimonials section, that is one section to skim past — not three places the reader stumbles into proof.

| Letter length | Minimum different signals | Spread across |
|---|---|---|
| 800 words | 5 | 3+ sections |
| 1,500 words | 7 | 4+ sections |
| 2,000+ words | 10 | 5+ sections |

Below the minimum, the letter reads as hype. If every signal is the same kind (e.g. nine testimonials and nothing else), the reader pattern-matches it as a curated highlight reel and discounts the lot.

## Common trust-density failures

We checked the 8 modern letters in our strong-exemplars set. The same gaps show up over and over:

- **Testimonials carry the whole load.** Brendon Luu's letter uses 2 video testimonials + 1 written. Roofgrow has 4 named case studies. Beyond those, the proof stack is thin. No demonstration. No admission. No verifiable fact about the process.
- **Demonstration is absent in 8 of 8.** Not one of the modern letters shows the machinery — no dashboard screenshots, no process walkthroughs, no "here is what the onboarding email looks like". Hopkins would have led with that.
- **Credentials-as-proof, not facts-as-proof.** "30 years on Wall Street" (Syncom 9yce), "founder + 150+ roofers framing" (Roofgrow). These are CV lines. Ogilvy did not say "we are luxury experts" — he said "5 coats of primer, 9 coats of finishing paint".
- **Round numbers where specific would do.** "500+ closings", "100s of clients", "$2M+ managed". Halbert's P.S. names a $2.00 single-unit price and $1.00 multi-unit price. Round numbers signal estimate; precise numbers signal record-keeping.
- **Claimed honesty, not demonstrated honesty.** Syncom 9yce uses the line "extremely honest & transparent funding terms" — a *claim* of honesty, not a demonstration. The classical move is to give something up (Karbo's 50-cent cost reveal). The modern move is to assert the trait and move on. See `damaging-admission.md` for the full pattern.

## What reviewers should check

1. **Are there at least 5 different *kinds* of signal in the letter?** Count the kinds, not the instances. Nine testimonials = one kind. *(yes / no)*
2. **Are the signals spread across at least 3 different sections?** Not stacked in one proof block. *(yes / no)*
3. **Is at least one signal a verifiable fact or demonstration, not a testimonial?** *(yes / no)*
4. **Are the numbers specific or rounded?** Score 1–5. 1 = "100s of clients". 5 = Halbert's "$2.00 single, $1.00 each additional" or Hopkins' "1,200 experiments".
5. **Does any confident claim sit unpaired?** Score 1–5. A confident claim (bold outcome, specific number) should sit next to a credibility signal — a constraint, a context, a condition — within the next 3 sentences.
6. **Is there any claimed honesty without demonstrated honesty?** Lines like "we are transparent" or "no hidden fees" without a giveaway behind them. *(yes / no)*

Checks 1 to 3 catch thin or mono-signal letters. Checks 4 to 5 catch the credibility-pairing gap. Check 6 catches trust theatre.
