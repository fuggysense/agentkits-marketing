# hopkins-schlitz — Established "demonstration of the obvious" as a category-winning move

**Source:** https://oncopy.substack.com/p/how-claude-hopkins-saved-schlitz + foolonahill.com/schiltz.html + Hopkins' own *My Life in Advertising* (1927)
**Author:** Claude C. Hopkins
**Year ran:** ~1919-1920s
**Format:** Full-page newspaper ad (originally tested in 1-2 markets, then scaled nationally)
**Word count:** ~280 (typical version)
**Vertical:** Consumer packaged goods (beer)
**Label:** STRONG (proven control)
**Proof of strength:** Schlitz went from 5th place to *tied 1st* in U.S. beer market in ~months. Hopkins built his methodology on this case study (along with Pepsodent, Goodyear, Palmolive). The "first to explain the obvious wins" principle is named after this campaign.

## Hook
- **Type:** Process-led headline (not benefit, not curiosity)
- **Approximate exact opening:** > "Could you but see how Schlitz beer is brewed, you would never buy any other."
- **Why it works:** Frames the reader as someone about to be *let in on a secret*. The construction "if only you could see X, you would Y" creates an open-loop visit — the body delivers the tour. Not a promise of product benefit; a promise of *visibility into process*.

## Lead type
- **Type:** Demonstration / behind-the-scenes
- **How it executes:** The ad reads like a factory tour transcript. "You would see plate-glass rooms... you would see immense filters... you would see..." The repetition of "you would see" is a recurring anaphoric structure that creates rhythm and embeds the reader in the visualization.

## Mechanism
- **Named:** NO (process not branded)
- **Justification:** The process IS the mechanism, listed as discrete steps. Modern equivalent would be naming it ("The Schlitz Pure-Brewing Process") but Hopkins didn't — and didn't need to. The *specifics* did the branding work.

## Proof types
- **Process detail:** plate-glass rooms, filtered air, white-wood pulp filtration
- **Frequency proof:** "every pump and pipe cleaned twice daily"
- **Quantity proof:** "every bottle washed four times by machinery", "4,000-foot artesian wells", "1,200 experiments"
- **Counter-intuitive proof:** "our brewery stands by the side of Lake Michigan" but we use deep-well water (rejects the convenient choice)
- **Reputation anchor:** "50-year reputation"
- **Zero testimonials, zero customer voice.** All authority comes from process specificity.

## CTA architecture
- **Style:** Recognition cue, not transaction
- **Urgency:** Zero
- **Friction:** Almost none — beer was sold everywhere; reader just needs to look for the Schlitz brand mark next purchase

## Guarantee variant
- **Type:** Reputation anchor
- **Specific terms:** "Backed by our 50-year reputation"
- **Note:** No formal money-back; CPG categories rarely use them. The factual proof IS the guarantee.

## P.S. section
- **Count:** ZERO P.S.
- **Function:** None
- **Why:** Print ad, not a letter. The closing line ("see that the cork or crown is branded Schlitz") functions as the recognition cue.

## Standout pattern
**Demonstration of the obvious.** Every brewery was doing the same purity-process steps. Every brewery was claiming "pure." Nobody had taken the reader *inside* the process. Hopkins won the entire category by being first to *show* the proof everyone else was claiming. The takeaway from Hopkins' tour conversation is the entire move: factory exec says "but every brewer does this" → Hopkins says "but no one has told the story." First-mover advantage on *explanation* is permanent — even if competitors later copied the move, Schlitz owned "purity" because they explained it first.

## Era-translation notes
- **Translates directly:** the process-as-proof move, the sensory-specific list (plate glass, white-wood pulp, live steam), the "first to explain wins" insight, the recognition-cue CTA.
- **Era-locked:** the printed-ad format, the "look for the brand mark" CTA (modern = "search 'Schlitz purity process' on YouTube"), the bottle-cap branding.
- **Modern equivalent:** Every "how it's made" content brand. Liquid Death's manufacturing transparency. Stripe's developer-experience-explanation pages. Tesla's gigafactory tours. The Hopkins technique IS the modern "build in public" / "factory tour" / "process content" movement.

## What modern letters (Stream A) are MISSING that this letter has
1. **Process-as-proof, not feature-as-benefit.** Hopkins' ad doesn't say "Schlitz is delicious" — he says "here's exactly how we make it." Stream A says "Get clarity, build confidence" without ever explaining the manufacturing process behind the result. Process detail is more persuasive than benefit claims because process is *checkable* and benefits are *projections*.
2. **First-mover-on-explanation positioning.** Hopkins owned "pure" not because Schlitz was actually purer but because they explained it first. Stream A doesn't think about *category linguistic ownership* — every coach uses the same vocabulary (clarity, scale, automation). Whoever defines a term first owns it. This is a missing strategic layer in Stream A.
3. **Anaphoric body structure.** "You would see... you would see... you would see..." creates rhythm and forces the reader into the visualization. Stream A bodies are list-of-disconnected-claims. Anaphora is a rhetorical move modern letters have lost.
4. **Counter-intuitive details ("we use deep wells even though Lake Michigan is right there").** Each detail signals over-investment. Stream A claims over-investment ("I obsessed over every detail") but never *demonstrates* it with a specific concrete tradeoff. The counter-intuitive choice is the strongest form of demonstration.
